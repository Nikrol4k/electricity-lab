The science

What is happening?

Four quantities

  • Voltage (U, volts): the electrical "push" the battery gives to charge.
  • Resistance (R, ohms): how strongly a component opposes the flow of current.
  • Current (I, amperes): how much charge flows past a point each second.
  • Power (P, watts): how fast electrical energy is converted into heat and light.

Three formulas

  • I = U ÷ R Ohm's law
  • P = U × I electrical power
  • P = U² ÷ R the same power, written with U and R only

A worked example

At 6 V across 10 Ω, the current is 0.60 A and the power is 3.60 W. Raise the voltage to 12 V with the same resistance: the current doubles to 1.20 A, but the power becomes 14.40 W, which is four times larger. Power depends on the square of the voltage, so a small voltage change makes a big difference to brightness.

Now keep 12 V and raise the resistance to 26 Ω. The current drops to about 0.46 A and the power to about 5.54 W, so the bulb dims.

Limits of this simulation

  • Constant resistance. Here, R stays fixed at the value you choose. A real incandescent bulb has a filament whose resistance rises sharply as it heats up, so a real bulb does not follow I = U ÷ R with one fixed number. A cold filament has much lower resistance than a hot one.
  • Ideal parts. The battery gives a perfectly steady voltage and the wires have no resistance. Real batteries and wires are not perfect.
  • Schematic brightness. The glow on screen is a visual guide to the calculated power. It is not a calibrated measurement of light output. Most of an incandescent bulb's power becomes heat, not light.

About this model

  • Constant resistance. Each bulb is treated as an ideal resistive load with a fixed resistance. A real incandescent filament changes resistance as it heats up (it is much lower when cold), so real bulbs only roughly follow these numbers. The simplification is fine for learning how series and parallel circuits behave.
  • Ideal battery and wires. The supply holds exactly the voltage you choose, and the wires have no resistance.
  • Conventional current. Arrows and dots show conventional current: the direction positive charge would move, from the + plate around the circuit to the − plate. In metal wires the moving charges are actually electrons, which drift the opposite way.
  • Schematic brightness. The glow is a visual guide to the calculated power, not a measurement of light output.

The science

What's the difference?

Series circuit

Components share one path. The same current flows through every component, while the supply voltage is divided between them.

Parallel circuit

Components have separate paths. Each branch receives the same supply voltage, while the total current is divided between branches.

Series and parallel circuits compared
Property Series Parallel
Current The same through every bulb: I = I1 = I2 Divides between branches: I = I1 + I2
Voltage Divided between the bulbs: U = U1 + U2 The same across every branch: U = U1 = U2
Total resistance Adds up: R = R1 + R2, larger than either bulb alone 1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2, smaller than either bulb alone
If one bulb fails The whole circuit stops. Both bulbs go out. The other bulb keeps working.

Quick knowledge check

Pick an answer to see whether it is right.

1. In a series circuit, what happens to the current?

2. In a parallel circuit, what happens to the voltage across each branch?

3. In a parallel circuit, one branch is disconnected. What happens to the bulb on the other branch?
