The science

What is happening?

Four quantities

  • Voltage (U, volts): the electrical "push" the battery gives to charge.
  • Resistance (R, ohms): how strongly a component opposes the flow of current.
  • Current (I, amperes): how much charge flows past a point each second.
  • Power (P, watts): how fast electrical energy is converted into heat and light.

Three formulas

  • I = U ÷ R Ohm's law
  • P = U × I electrical power
  • P = U² ÷ R the same power, written with U and R only

A worked example

At 6 V across 10 Ω, the current is 0.60 A and the power is 3.60 W. Raise the voltage to 12 V with the same resistance: the current doubles to 1.20 A, but the power becomes 14.40 W, which is four times larger. Power depends on the square of the voltage, so a small voltage change makes a big difference to brightness.

Now keep 12 V and raise the resistance to 26 Ω. The current drops to about 0.46 A and the power to about 5.54 W, so the bulb dims.

Limits of this simulation

  • Constant resistance. Here, R stays fixed at the value you choose. A real incandescent bulb has a filament whose resistance rises sharply as it heats up, so a real bulb does not follow I = U ÷ R with one fixed number. A cold filament has much lower resistance than a hot one.
  • Ideal parts. The battery gives a perfectly steady voltage and the wires have no resistance. Real batteries and wires are not perfect.
  • Schematic brightness. The glow on screen is a visual guide to the calculated power. It is not a calibrated measurement of light output. Most of an incandescent bulb's power becomes heat, not light.
